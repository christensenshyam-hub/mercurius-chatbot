Here's something that might surprise you: the word "bank" — whether you mean a riverbank or a place to store money — becomes the **same token** inside a language model.

Let's start at the very beginning: what happens the instant you hit send.

Your prompt doesn't enter the model as words. It gets chopped into **tokens** — chunks that are roughly (but not exactly) words. "Unhappiness" might become three tokens: "un", "happi", "ness".

A single emoji might be one token. A space before a word sometimes becomes part of the next token.

[KEY]Tokenization converts your text into numbered chunks before the model ever "reads" a single word.[/KEY]

[EX]GPT-4's tokenizer works from a vocabulary of about 100,000 tokens. Each token maps to a number — so your whole prompt becomes a list of integers before anything else happens.[/EX]

Exactly right — your prompt becomes a list of numbers before the model does anything else with it.

Now here's where it gets interesting. Those numbers on their own don't carry meaning. "Bank" (money) and "bank" (river) get the same token number — same integer, every time.

So how does the model ever know which meaning fits?

That's where the **attention mechanism** comes in.

As the model processes your token list, it weighs each token against every other token in the prompt. In "I went to the bank to fish," the word "fish" pulls attention toward "bank" and nudges the model toward the river meaning — not the financial one.

[KEY]Attention lets the model figure out which other tokens are most relevant to each token, so the same word can mean different things in different sentences.[/KEY]

[EX]In "I went to the bank to deposit my check," attention links "deposit" and "check" to "bank" — pushing toward the financial meaning. Swap in "fish" and "river" and the same mechanism flips the interpretation.[/EX]

Close, but not quite — and this is a really common mix-up worth clearing up.

The model *did* train on sentences containing "bank." But memorizing those sentences isn't what solves the problem. If it just memorized, it couldn't handle a brand-new sentence it had never seen before.

What actually happens is that attention runs **live**, on your specific prompt, every single time. It looks at which tokens are near "bank" *right now* and weighs them against each other in the moment.

That's what makes it flexible — not a stored list of sentences, but a mechanism that reads context fresh each time.

Yes — that's the one. Attention reads the tokens around a word in real time, every single time, which is what lets the same token mean different things in different contexts.

Now for the final piece: what does the model actually *do* with all of that?

After tokenization and attention, the model produces a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary — roughly 100,000 tokens — and picks the most likely next one. Then it does it again. And again.

That's **next-token prediction**: the whole output is built one token at a time, each choice informed by everything that came before.

[KEY]An LLM generates text by repeatedly asking "given everything so far, what token comes next?" — it never plans the whole sentence in advance.[/KEY]

[EX]Given "The cat sat on the ___", the model assigns high probability to "mat", "floor", "couch" — and very low probability to "democracy" or "seventeen". It picks from that distribution, outputs a token, and repeats.[/EX]
