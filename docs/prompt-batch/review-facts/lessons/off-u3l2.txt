Now let's look at what happens when an AI tool gets *widely deployed* in hospitals — and the gap between how it performs on paper versus in the real world.

Epic's **Sepsis Prediction Model** is built into hospital software used across the US. Sepsis is a life-threatening emergency, so catching it early matters enormously.

When Michigan Medicine tested it on their own patients (Wong et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2021), the results were sobering.

[EX]The model missed about **two-thirds** of sepsis cases. And roughly **88% of its alerts were false alarms** — meaning for every 12 real sepsis patients it flagged, it also flagged about 88 people who didn't have it. It alerted on 18% of all hospitalized patients. That's the recipe for **alert fatigue**: nurses and doctors start ignoring alarms because most of them are wrong.[/EX]

[KEY]A model can look promising in development but fail in deployment — and when false alarms are constant, clinicians stop trusting the alerts, which defeats the whole purpose.[/KEY]

Epic disputed the study's definition of sepsis, which is a real methodological debate — but the alert fatigue problem it revealed is widely recognized in healthcare AI.

Now for the third piece of this lesson, and it's the most subtle one.

---

Sometimes the problem isn't that the AI fails to predict — it's that it's predicting *the wrong thing*.

A widely used commercial algorithm helped hospitals decide which patients needed extra care. Sounds straightforward. But here's what it was actually trained to predict: **past healthcare costs**, not health needs.

[EX]Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients — due to systemic barriers to care, not because they were healthier — the algorithm rated them as lower-risk than equally sick white patients. Fixing it would have raised the share of Black patients flagged for extra care from **17.7% to 46.5%** (Obermeyer et al., *Science*, 2019).[/EX]

[KEY]When a proxy measure (like cost) stands in for the real target (like health need), bias in the proxy becomes bias in the AI's decisions — invisibly.[/KEY]

The algorithm wasn't trained to discriminate. It was trained on a flawed stand-in for what actually mattered.
