Here's something that might surprise you: the AI reading your message right now has never seen a single *word*.

It sees numbers. Only numbers.

When you type a prompt, the very first thing that happens is your text gets sliced into chunks called **tokens** — and those chunks aren't always whole words.

[KEY]A token is the basic unit an LLM reads: sometimes a whole word, sometimes just part of one, sometimes punctuation.[/KEY]

[EX]The word "tokenization" might become three tokens: "token", "ization" — or even smaller pieces, depending on the model. Then each token gets swapped for a number from the model's vocabulary. GPT-4's tokenizer has a vocabulary of about 100,000 of these numbered tokens.[/EX]

So your beautifully written sentence arrives inside the model as a list of integers. No letters. No spaces.

Just numbers in a sequence.

Exactly right — the words are gone before the model even starts "thinking."

Now, those numbers don't just sit in a list. The model needs to know what each token *means* — and meaning, it turns out, is about **relationships**.

Each token gets mapped to a point in a giant mathematical space, where tokens with similar meanings land near each other. "King" and "queen" are close. "Bank" (money) and "bank" (river) get the *same* token — but context will sort them out in a moment.

[KEY]An embedding turns each token-number into a rich point in meaning-space, capturing how that token relates to every other token the model has ever learned from.[/KEY]

[EX]In the sentence "I went to the bank," the word "bank" gets one token — but the surrounding words "went," "to," and "the" nudge the model toward the financial meaning rather than the riverbank meaning. That nudging is the attention mechanism at work: each token "looks at" the others to figure out what it should mean *here*.[/EX]

So the model isn't reading left to right like you are. It's weighing every token against every other token simultaneously.

Actually, that's the tricky part — the tokenizer doesn't know anything about meaning. It just slices text into chunks and assigns numbers mechanically. "Bank" gets the same token number every single time, no matter what it means.

It's the **attention mechanism** — later in the process — that looks at surrounding words and figures out which meaning fits.

Think of it this way: the tokenizer is like a scanner that just photographs letters. The attention mechanism is the reader who actually understands the sentence.

Exactly — the attention mechanism is the part that actually "reads" context. The tokenizer just scans; attention understands.

Now you have the full pipeline: tokens → embeddings → attention → and finally, the part that produces your answer.

After attention has figured out what everything means in context, the model does one thing repeatedly: it **predicts the next token**.

Not the whole answer at once. One token at a time, over and over.

[KEY]Next-token prediction means the model picks the single most probable next chunk of text, outputs it, then uses the updated sequence to predict the one after that.[/KEY]

[EX]Finish this: "The cat sat on the ___." Almost everyone thinks "mat" or "floor" — and so does the model, because those completions appear overwhelmingly often in training text. High confidence. But try "The election results were surprisingly ___" — now dozens of words fit equally well ("close," "dramatic," "disappointing"), so confidence is low and small random variation decides the pick.[/EX]

This is why AI can sound fluent and still be wrong: it's optimizing for *plausibility*, not truth.
