Even a well-tested tool can fail badly when it's deployed in the real world — and the failure mode isn't always obvious.

**Alert fatigue** is one of the most dangerous: when an AI flags too many false alarms, clinicians start ignoring alerts — including the real ones.

[EX]Michigan Medicine tested Epic's Sepsis Model on its own patients (Wong et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021). It missed about two-thirds of sepsis cases. About 88% of its alerts were false alarms. It flagged 18% of *all* hospitalized patients — so staff were drowning in warnings. Epic disputed the study's definition of sepsis.[/EX]

[KEY]Alert fatigue happens when a model cries wolf so often that clinicians tune it out — turning a tool meant to save lives into background noise.[/KEY]

You got it — the car alarm analogy is exactly right. Constant false alarms train people to stop reacting, and that's when the real danger slips through.

Now for the third piece of this lesson — and it's the sneakiest failure mode of all.

---

Sometimes an AI doesn't fail because it's inaccurate. It fails because it's measuring the **wrong thing**.

When you train a model to predict "who needs more care," you have to pick something measurable as your target. The choice of that target can bake in bias invisibly.

[EX]A widely used commercial algorithm (Obermeyer et al., Science, 2019) used past healthcare *cost* as a stand-in for health *need*. Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients, the model rated them as healthier than equally sick white patients. Fixing it would raise the share of Black patients flagged for extra care from 17.7% to 46.5%.[/EX]

[KEY]When an AI is trained to predict a flawed proxy — like cost instead of need — it can systematically disadvantage entire groups, even with no intent to discriminate.[/KEY]
