```text
Here's something that should unsettle you a little:

The same feature that makes AI writing sound polished and authoritative is *exactly* what makes it dangerous.

**Fluency** is baked into how these models work — they're trained to produce text that sounds like good, confident writing. But "sounds good" and "is true" are completely different things, and the model has no way to check the difference.

Here's the core idea: an LLM doesn't retrieve facts from a database. It predicts the next most plausible token, over and over. "Plausible" means *statistically likely given the training data* — not *verified against reality*.

[KEY]LLMs optimize for plausible-sounding text, not for truth — so confident tone and factual accuracy are independent of each other.[/KEY]

[EX]In 2023, two lawyers submitted a legal brief in *Mata v. Avianca* that cited multiple court cases — complete with case names, docket numbers, and legal reasoning. ChatGPT had invented every single one. A federal judge fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000. The AI didn't flag uncertainty. It didn't hedge. It wrote like it knew.[/EX]

B) It predicted text that looked like real legal citations, because that's what plausible legal writing contains
C) Its training data didn't include any real court cases
D) It searched the internet and found incorrect information
ANS: B
[/Q]
```

```text
Exactly right — the model wasn't lying, it was *completing a pattern*. Legal citations have a very recognizable shape, and the model is very good at producing that shape, whether or not anything real is behind it.

Now here's the second piece, and it's a bit counterintuitive.

You might assume a more polished, fine-tuned assistant would be *better* at knowing what it doesn't know. But the OpenAI GPT-4 Technical Report (2023) found the opposite: the raw pre-trained model's confidence actually matched its accuracy reasonably well — but post-training (including RLHF, the human-feedback process) made that **calibration** worse.

The assistant got more helpful-sounding. It also got more confidently wrong.

[KEY]Calibration means a model's expressed confidence matches its actual accuracy — and fine-tuning for helpfulness can quietly break that.[/KEY]

[EX]Think of it this way: human raters reward answers that sound clear and decisive. So the model learns to sound clear and decisive — even in situations where "I'm not sure" would be the honest answer.[/EX]

ANS: B
```
