```text
Not quite — actually, many companies do test their datasets, and bias still slips through.

That's exactly what makes it so tricky: even careful teams miss it, because the skewed patterns *look like reality*. If history produced mostly male engineers, a dataset full of male engineers doesn't throw up any red flags — it just looks accurate.

Now let's go one stage further — what happens inside the model itself.

Even with perfectly balanced data, the choices engineers make about *what to optimize for* can introduce bias.

Say you're building a loan-approval model and you tell it to maximize profit. That sounds neutral. But if certain zip codes have historically had lower repayment rates — partly because of decades of discriminatory lending — the model learns to penalize those zip codes.

The optimization target was "profit," not "discriminate," but the outcome is discriminatory.

[KEY]Algorithmic bias comes from the choices made about what the model is trying to achieve — not just what data it learned from.[/KEY]

[EX]Imagine a school district builds a tutoring-recommendation tool trained only on students who already had internet access at home. The model learns that "engaged learner" looks like someone with fast homework turnaround — a pattern that disadvantages students without reliable internet, even after those students get devices.[/EX]
```

```text
Exactly right — and that's a sharp example.

"Staying longest" sounds like a neutral goal, but it ends up penalizing people who take parental leave, move for a spouse's job, or have caregiving responsibilities. The model didn't mean to discriminate against parents — it just learned that certain life patterns predict shorter tenure.

That's **algorithmic bias**: the optimization target shapes what the model rewards, and those rewards can land unevenly across groups.

Now for the third stage — deployment.

A model can be built carefully and still cause harm when it's used in the wrong context.

[KEY]Deployment bias happens when a model is applied to a population, setting, or purpose it wasn't designed or tested for.[/KEY]

[EX]Imagine a skin-condition screening tool trained almost entirely on patients at urban research hospitals. It works well there — but when a rural clinic starts using it on an older, less diverse patient population, accuracy drops sharply. Nobody changed the model. The context changed.[/EX]
```
