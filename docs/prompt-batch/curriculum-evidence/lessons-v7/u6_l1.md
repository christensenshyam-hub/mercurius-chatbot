Here's something that might surprise you: AI image generators don't work like collages.

They don't cut pieces from real photos and stitch them together. They start from pure static — random noise — and sculpt it into an image from scratch.

That's the core idea behind **diffusion models**.

[KEY]A diffusion model starts with random noise and gradually "denoises" it, guided by a text prompt, until a coherent image appears.[/KEY]

[EX]Think of it like a sculptor starting with a block of marble — except the "marble" is random static, and the prompt ("a red fox in a snowy forest") is the blueprint. Tools like Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, and ChatGPT's image generator all work this way.[/EX]

The model runs this denoising process in dozens of steps, nudging the image a little closer to "matches the prompt" each time — until something realistic emerges that never existed before.

That's actually the most common misconception about AI images — so it's a great one to clear up!

The answer is **C — random noise**.

Diffusion models don't have a photo library they search through. They generate brand-new pixels from scratch, starting from pure static. That's why AI images can show things that have never been photographed — impossible lighting, fictional creatures, scenes that don't exist.

"Stitching real photos" is how some older tools worked, but not how modern generators like Midjourney or Stable Diffusion operate.

Now let's add the second piece: **voice cloning**.

Voice cloning works on a similar "learn the pattern, then generate" principle — but for audio instead of pixels.

[KEY]Modern voice cloning can copy someone's voice from just a few seconds of audio sample, then generate new speech in that voice saying anything.[/KEY]

[EX]Microsoft's VALL-E research model (2023) claimed it could clone a voice from just 3 seconds of audio. The clone captures tone, accent, and speaking rhythm — not a recording of the person, but a newly generated imitation.[/EX]

The same logic extends to video: tools like Sora and Veo generate video frames from text prompts, the same noise-to-image process running across many frames in sequence.
