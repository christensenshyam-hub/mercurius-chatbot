```text
Here's something that might surprise you: giving students a better AI tutor made their practice scores *worse* in the long run — at least at first glance.

Wait, that's not quite right. Let me set it up properly.

---

Researchers gave nearly 1,000 high-school students in Turkey access to a plain GPT-4 chatbot while practicing math.

Their practice scores shot up **48%**.

Then the AI was taken away — and those students scored *17% lower* on the actual exam than students who had never used AI at all.

---

The students hadn't learned math. They'd learned how to ask a chatbot for math answers.

That's **cognitive offloading** — letting the AI do the thinking so your brain never has to struggle with the material.

---

The struggle is the point. When your brain has to *retrieve* something, work through confusion, or make mistakes and fix them, that effort is what builds a lasting memory. Psychologists call it "desirable difficulty."

Hand the difficulty to AI, and you skip the very thing that makes learning stick.

[KEY]Cognitive offloading feels productive in the moment but removes the struggle that actually builds learning.[/KEY]
```

```text

The chatbot wasn't giving wrong answers. It was giving *correct* ones — and that was the problem.

When the AI solved the problems, the students' brains never had to struggle. No struggle, no lasting memory. The exam just exposed what was never really learned.

---

Now here's where it gets interesting: the same study tested a *different* kind of AI tutor.

Instead of handing over answers, this version gave **hints** — nudging students toward the next step without doing the thinking for them.

Practice scores went up even more (127%), and those students didn't crash on the exam the way the plain-chatbot group did.

---

The difference is who's doing the cognitive work.

"Just give me the answer" → AI thinks, you copy.

"Explain why my approach is wrong" or "give me a hint" → *you* think, AI supports.

[KEY]The productive pattern is: use AI to explain, generate practice, and give hints — then retrieve and apply the idea yourself.[/KEY]

[EX]Instead of "solve this equation for me," try "I got x = 5 but the answer is x = 3 — where did I go wrong?" Now you're driving; the AI is a coach.[/EX]
```
