The thing blocking 3x is the **+ 5** on the same side.

To undo adding 5, you subtract 5 from both sides — that's the rule of **inverse operations**: whatever you do to one side, you mirror on the other, so the equation stays balanced.

That gives you: 3x = 20 − 5.

--- USER: [Explain more chip, responseMode deep]
**Inverse operations** work because an equation is a balance scale — both sides must stay equal.

When you subtract 5 from the left, the + 5 and − 5 cancel to zero, leaving 3x alone. When you subtract 5 from the right, 20 becomes 15. The scale stays balanced.

This same logic works for any operation:
- Addition is undone by subtraction
- Multiplication is undone by division
- Squaring is undone by a square root

So once you have 3x = 15, the 3 is *multiplying* x — which means the inverse move is dividing both sides by 3.

What do you get when you divide both sides by 3?

==================== run 2 alg-explain-more [socratic] 

--- USER: what is 3x+5 = 20
What's your first move to get 3x by itself?

--- USER: [Explain more chip, responseMode deep]
The key idea is **inverse operations** — every math operation has an opposite that "undoes" it.

Addition and subtraction are inverses. Multiplication and division are inverses.

In 3x + 5 = 20, the + 5 is "attached" to the 3x side. To remove it, you use its inverse: subtract 5 from both sides.

The reason you do it to BOTH sides is like a balance scale — whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other to keep it equal.

So: if you subtract 5 from both sides of 3x + 5 = 20, what equation do you get?

--- USER: [Explain more chip, responseMode deep]
The "both sides" rule comes from the **equals sign** itself — it's a claim that two expressions have the same value.

The moment you change one side without changing the other, the claim becomes false. You haven't solved the equation; you've broken it.

Think of it like this: if two people have the same amount of money and you take $5 from only one of them, they no longer have equal amounts. The equality is gone.

This is why every algebraic step is really a *logical argument* — "if these two things are equal, and I do the same thing to both, they're still equal." Each step preserves the truth of the equation.

There's a name for this in formal math: the **properties of equality** — the addition property, subtraction property, multiplication property, and division property. Each one says the same thing: identical operations on both sides keep the equation balanced.

The deeper idea is that solving an equation is really a chain of "if…then" statements. You're not *finding* x so much as *proving* what x must be, step by step, while keeping the equation true at every line.

Now — back to yours: if you subtract 5 from both sides of 3x + 5 = 20, what do you get on each side?
